Q: what does an electron look like?


A: first you have to define what you mean by that.  Usually when we "look at" something, some photons from a light or the sun hit the thing we want to see, then they go to our eyes, which senses the photons.  (The photons really go off in all directions, but only the lucky ones are aimed at our eyes.)


Imagine a boat in a lake.  We can splash some waves toward it, and the waves bounce off and come back.  By checking out the waves that get reflected, we can figure out how big the boat is, and the shape of its surface (at the water line).  This will also work with rocks or posts in the water - anything that the waves bounce off.


The problem is, if our boat is a small toy boat, all we can tell is that there's something there that's reflecting waves.  It shows up as a large, hazy dot.  That's because the waves are larger than the boat itself.  And so that boat will look like a rock the same size, which will look like a post of the same size.  We might not even be able to see the dot if it's too small.


The light that our eyes see are waves, and they have wavelengths of 400 to 700 nanometers, that's 4 x 10<sup>-7</sup> to 7 x 10<sup>7</sup> meters.  That's very tiny, about 20 millionths of an inch.  It's great for us humans to look at large things, and even tiny things, but anything smaller than those waves, we won't be able to see much.  An atom can be as small as 10<sup>-10</sup> meters, that's more than 1000 times smaller.  So we can't see them with light.  In fact, we can barely see them with electron microscopes.





Quantum mechanical sizes are very different from the sizes that you and I are used to.  They're so tiny, that some things we're familiar with, just doesn't happen.  For instance, there's no friction - friction comes from atoms in the two surfaces trying to bond to each other ... and then getting ripped apart.  Atoms usually bond using their electrons.  A single electron typically lives wrapped around a nucleus in an atom.  There's little or nothing to slow it down.

## stupid atoms

There's this cartoon image of an atom that, I'm sure, you've seen before.  Drives me crazy.  Sometimes, those electron balls are as big as the neutrons and protons in the center.  Those stupid rings that represent the path of each electron — no.
There's no rings.
The electrons do orbit, but the details are all different.
![stupid atom]{stupidAtom.jpg}
This one's all jazzed up with glowing stuff and shine as if there's lights down there.

First of all, you see stuff with photons — packets of light.
They're waves, and their wavelengths (cycle lengths) are tiny, 400 to 700 nanometers (.000 400 to .000 700 millimeter)*.
The atoms, themselves, are about 100 to 200 picometers (.000 000 100 millimeter) — a thousand times smaller.
So, it's like trying to find a pinhead, by going around the room, and trying to bounce a basketball off of it.

But, we know something about atoms and we can describe it geometrically, even if our photons are too big and clumsy to use.

## Atoms

Almost all of the size of an atom is its electron shells.
The electrons would be flying all around, except the nucleus has protons,
that attract the electrons, and mostly keep them in their orbits.
The electrons are still wispy waves,
and their orbits have to be a whole number of wavelengths in circumference.

Atoms usually have as many electrons as their nucleus has protons.
This usually makes the whole atom electrically neutral, not attracting nor repelling other atoms.
But, the outer electrons are the easiest to distract.
Atoms can share or trade those electrons to make compounds, or to make solids or liquids.
This is a whole topic named Chemistry.

The nucleus is about 100,000 times smaller than the whole atom's size.
But, it's got almost all the mass; electrons are 1/2000 of the mass of neutrons or protons.
This is a whole topic named Nuclear Physics.

So, the atom is kindof like a balloon with a tiny lead weight in the middle,
suspended with rubber bands.
They can bounce off each other, with or without bonding.
Unlike rubber bands, they can keep on vibrating, forever.
The energy there is called Heat.
